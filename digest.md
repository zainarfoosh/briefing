## Script

Good morning, Zain. Today is a heavier one, led by two separate safety stories that matter more than any deal on the sheet, then a run of financings and one genuinely good clinical readout.

Start with the hardest news. STAT reports a second child has died in a CRISPR gene editing trial in China, run by a Shanghai based startup testing a therapy in a boy with muscular dystrophy. This happened under loosened regulatory oversight, and it lands on a modality that has already been under scrutiny after prior in vivo editing safety scares. Expect this to sharpen questions about how China's faster but thinner review process for gene editing trials gets treated by Western partners and acquirers, and it is the kind of event that can cool enrollment and financing across the whole editing space, not just this one program.

Separately, a group of Prader-Willi syndrome experts has sent clinicians a letter warning about possible safety issues with Neurocrine's Vykat XR. Seven patients prescribed the drug have died since its approval last year, based on the FDA's adverse event database. The deaths aren't definitively linked to the drug, but the experts' concern is that patients and physicians haven't been adequately warned. This is early, but a postmarket safety letter from an independent expert group, not the company or the FDA, is unusual, and it puts pressure on Neurocrine to respond before this becomes a label fight.

On a better note, Definium Therapeutics said its oral LSD candidate cleared the bar in the first of two phase three trials in generalized anxiety disorder, just two months after a positive readout in a severe form of depression. Two independent phase three wins for the same molecule in two different indications is a real derisking event, and it strengthens the case that this psychedelic platform has a broad, repeatable mechanism rather than one lucky trial.

On the deal and financing front, Georg Schett, the CAR-T researcher known for resetting immune systems in autoimmune disease, launched Boulevard Bio with sixty five million dollars, built around three antibody candidates including one licensed out of China that puts it in direct competition with Vera Therapeutics' IgA nephropathy drug. Betting on off the shelf antibodies rather than one-at-a-time CAR-T is Schett's attempt to make his own science scalable, and it's a signal that IgA nephropathy is becoming a crowded, well capitalized fight.

Elsewhere, AbCellera is raising around two hundred million dollars in a stock offering after hitting its mark in a phase two hot flash trial, a classic case of a company monetizing a positive readout immediately through the public markets rather than waiting. Silence and InduPro also raised significant rounds this week, and on the earlier stage side, Epicrispr took in ninety million dollars off early epigenetic data in FSHD, while Vaderis landed a hundred and fifty two million dollars to fund a phase three in a rare bleeding disorder, both signs that investors are still willing to write large single-asset checks when the data clears an early bar.

On the licensing side, Lilly paid two billion dollars upfront for vaccine biotechs, an unusually large upfront relative to typical milestone-heavy vaccine deals, which tells you Lilly wants this asset now rather than betting on future data. And AbbVie, Novartis, and Bristol Myers Squibb filed lawsuits in Illinois over the 340B drug discount program, adding another state to the industry's ongoing fight over how that program gets administered.

Worth reading deeper is STAT's investigation into Commure, the seven billion dollar startup racing to automate the business side of health care. It's a useful read on what happens when a well funded platform company prioritizes speed over customer trust, and why that tension eventually shows up in its unit economics.
